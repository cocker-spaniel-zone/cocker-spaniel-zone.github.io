Why Do Cocker Spaniels Get Ear Infections So Often?

Why do Cocker Spaniels get ear infections so often? Learn the early signs, the grooming mistakes that make it worse, and how owners can lower the odds.

Home›Blog›Why Do Cocker Spaniels Get Ear Infections So Often?

Published: 2026-05-21 · Cocker Spaniel Zone

Why do Cocker Spaniels get ear infections more than almost any other breed? The short answer is a triple threat: the floppy, heavy ear flap creates a dark, warm, humid tunnel; their ear canals are unusually narrow; and many Cockers have underlying allergies that trigger inflammation inside the ear. This combination turns a normal amount of ear wax and moisture into a perfect breeding ground for bacteria and yeast. Understanding this is the first step to breaking the cycle for your dog.

Quick answer: Cocker Spaniel ear infections are mostly caused by the breed’s unique ear anatomy (long heavy flaps that trap moisture, narrow vertical ear canals) plus a high rate of skin allergies that cause inflammation deep in the ear. You can reduce the risk by keeping the ears dry, cleaning them gently but consistently, and treating allergies early with your vet.

1. The Anatomy Problem: Why a Cocker’s Ear Is a Trap

The Cocker Spaniel’s beautiful, silky ears are the breed’s signature feature, but they are also the root cause of repeated infections. Unlike a breed with upright ears, the Cocker’s long, pendulous ear flap (the pinna) covers the ear opening almost completely. This creates a dark, stagnant pocket of air that is warmer and more humid than the outside environment — perfect conditions for microbes to multiply.

More importantly, your Cocker Spaniel’s ear canal is shaped differently. It drops straight down vertically before turning horizontally toward the eardrum. This “L-shape” is much steeper and more narrow than in many other dogs. Debris, wax, and moisture settle at the bottom of that vertical canal and cannot drain out naturally because gravity works against the horizontal turn. Over time, this material builds up and starts to ferment, causing inflammation and infection deep where a cotton ball cannot reach.

The breed also produces more cerumen (ear wax) than average. This sticky, waxy substance is meant to protect the ear, but in the enclosed Cocker ear, it quickly becomes a food source for yeast and bacteria. When you combine a narrow vertical canal, a thick wax buildup, and poor air circulation, the odds of an infection are extremely high.

2. Moisture: The Catalyst Your Cocker Does Not Need

Water is the single most common trigger for an acute ear infection in a Cocker Spaniel. Moisture enters the ear canal and gets trapped under the ear flap, raising the humidity in that pocket even higher. Yeast, specifically Malassezia, thrives in wet, warm conditions. So does Pseudomonas, a stubborn bacteria that causes painful, foul-smelling infections.

The most obvious source of moisture is swimming or bathing. Many Cockers love water, whether it is a lake, a pool, or a backyard sprinkler. Every splash forces water into the ear canal, and because of the L-shaped anatomy, that water often stays there. A Cocker that swims weekly without ear drying is almost guaranteed to develop an infection within a few months.

But moisture can also come from less obvious sources. Living in a humid climate, even without swimming, raises the baseline moisture trapped under the ear flap. Some Cockers also have a tendency to drool or rub their wet faces on the ground, and that moisture can creep into the ear opening. Even a bath at home, if not followed by thorough ear drying, can tip the balance from healthy to infected.

What to do: After any water exposure — swimming, bathing, or even a rainy walk — dry the external ear flap with a soft towel. Then use a vet-approved drying solution (never human hydrogen peroxide) to help evaporate moisture inside the vertical canal. Gently hold the ear flap up for a minute afterward to let air circulate.

3. Allergies: The Hidden Driver of Chronic Ear Infections

If you are battling ear infection after ear infection despite good ear cleaning habits, your Cocker Spaniel likely has an underlying allergy. This is the number one root cause of recurrent otitis in the breed. Allergies cause inflammation in the skin, including the delicate skin lining the ear canal. Inflamed skin produces more wax and becomes thicker, narrowing the canal even further. This creates a vicious cycle: inflammation, more wax, poor drainage, more infection, more inflammation.

The most common allergies in Cocker Spaniels are food allergies (often to chicken, beef, dairy, or grains) and environmental allergies (dust mites, pollens, molds). Almost 50% of Cocker Spaniels with skin allergies will present with ear infections as one of the first symptoms, sometimes before any skin itching is obvious.

If your Cocker has red, greasy ears with a yeasty smell (like corn chips or bread) that keep coming back despite treatment, ask your veterinarian about allergy testing. Managing the allergy — whether through a novel protein diet, Apoquel, Cytopoint, or immunotherapy — is often the only way to stop the ear infections from returning. Treating the ear alone without addressing allergies is like mopping the floor while the sink is still overflowing.

4. Grooming Mistakes That Put Your Cocker at Risk

Many well-meaning owners actually make ear infections worse through incorrect grooming. The Cocker Spaniel has hair that grows inside the ear canal, not just on the outer flap. This hair traps wax and debris, and when it gets matted or wet, it forms a plug that blocks the canal completely.

Common grooming mistakes:

  • Plucking ear hair incorrectly: Plucking can cause micro-tears in the ear canal skin if done too aggressively, introducing bacteria deep into the tissue. If you pluck, use a gentle twisting motion with your fingers, not forceps, and only remove loose hair. Some vets recommend leaving the hair if it is not trapping debris.
  • Using cotton swabs: Never insert Q-tips or cotton swabs into the ear canal. They push wax and debris deeper into the L-shaped canal, compacting it at the turn. This is the fastest way to cause an impaction and a painful infection.
  • Over-cleaning or using harsh solutions: Cleaning the ear every day strips away protective oils and can cause irritation. Stick to cleaning once every 1-2 weeks, or more often only if directed by your vet. Use only products labeled for canine ears — human ear drops or alcohol can burn inflamed tissue.

The right way to clean: Fill the ear canal with a vet-recommended cleaning solution, gently massage the base of the ear for 30 seconds, then let your dog shake its head. The shaking throws out loosened debris from the vertical canal. Wipe away only what comes out onto the outer flap with a cotton ball. Do not dig inside.

Ear hair removal Gentle hand plucking of loose hairs every month Harsh plucking or shaving inside the canal with a clipper

5. Early Warning Signs: What to Look For Before It Gets Bad

Most Cocker Spaniel owners only notice an ear infection when the smell is obvious or the dog is shaking its head constantly. By that point, the infection is moderate to severe. Catching it early can save weeks of treatment and prevent the ear canal from becoming thickened and scarred — a condition called chronic otitis that can lead to hearing loss.

Subtle early signs to watch for:

  • Your dog rubs its ear against the carpet, furniture, or your leg
  • A slight increase in head shaking, especially after sleeping or eating
  • A waxy discharge that looks like coffee grounds (yeast) or pus (bacteria)
  • A faint, sweet, or musty odor when you lean close to the ear
  • Redness or swelling on the inside of the ear flap
  • Your dog flinches or pulls away when you touch the base of the ear
  • A “greasy” feel to the ear flap when you rub it between thumb and finger

If you see any one of these signs, start a gentle cleaning and drying routine immediately. If the symptoms do not improve within 48 hours, or if the ear becomes painful, smelly, or produces thick discharge, schedule a vet visit.

When to see a vet urgently:

  • Your dog cannot open its mouth or eat without pain
  • There is a head tilt, loss of balance, or circling
  • The ear flap is hot, swollen, and the dog cries when touched
  • You see blood or a dark, tarry discharge
  • Your dog has been shaking its head so hard that the ear flap is bruised (hematoma)

These signs can indicate a middle or inner ear infection, a ruptured eardrum, or a hematoma requiring drainage. Do not attempt home treatment for these.

6. When Home Care Is Not Enough: Treatment Realities

Home prevention and early cleaning work well for healthy ears, but once an infection is established, it almost always requires veterinary medication. Yeast and bacteria in Cocker Spaniel ears can be resistant to over-the-counter products.

Your veterinarian will likely perform an otoscopic exam to look deep into the vertical canal and check the eardrum. They may take a swab to determine whether the infection is yeast, bacteria, or both (mixed infections are common in Cockers). Medications usually include prescription ear drops containing antifungal and antibiotic agents, and often a corticosteroid to reduce inflammation and pain.

For chronic infections, the vet may recommend a deep ear flush under sedation. This is a thorough cleaning of the entire vertical and horizontal canal while your dog is asleep. It removes impacted debris that cannot be reached by home cleaning and gives the topical medication a clean surface to work on. In severe cases, where the canal has thickened irreversibly, a surgical procedure called a lateral ear resection may be considered, which opens the vertical canal to improve drainage.

If your Cocker has had three or more infections in a year, ask your vet about referral to a veterinary dermatologist. These specialists are experts in allergy management and chronic otitis, and they can offer treatments like ear cytology (examining cells from the ear) or long-term ear maintenance plans that are far more effective than general practice approaches.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I clean my Cocker Spaniel’s ears?

For a Cocker with no history of infections, once every 1-2 weeks is sufficient. If your dog has allergies or a history of ear infections, increase to once weekly or as recommended by your vet. Do not over-clean, as this can irritate the ear canal. Always use a veterinarian-recommended ear cleaner.

Can a Cocker Spaniel’s ear infection go away on its own?

No. Once an infection is established, the anatomy of the Cocker ear makes it impossible for the infection to clear without treatment. The warm, moist environment under the flap allows bacteria and yeast to continue multiplying. Left untreated, ear infections can become chronic and lead to thickening of the ear canal, hearing loss, or even a ruptured eardrum.

What foods cause ear infections in Cocker Spaniels?

Food allergies are a common trigger. Chicken, beef, dairy, wheat, and soy are the most frequent culprits. If you suspect a food allergy, talk to your vet about a 8-12 week food trial using a novel protein (like rabbit or venison) or a hydrolyzed protein diet. You should see a reduction in ear and skin inflammation within a few weeks if food is the cause.

Is it safe to use hydrogen peroxide in my Cocker’s ear?

No. Hydrogen peroxide can damage the delicate skin inside the ear canal and cause irritation, inflammation, and pain. It also dries out the ear tissue, which can lead to more wax production. Stick to veterinary-approved ear cleaning solutions that are formulated to be gentle and effective for dogs.

Why does my Cocker Spaniel shake its head after cleaning?

Head shaking after cleaning is normal. It is your dog’s instinctive way to expel loosened debris and excess fluid from the ear canal. This is actually helpful, as it flushes out the material that your cleaning solution loosened. Just wipe away any discharge that reaches the outer ear flap with a cotton ball afterward.

Can ear infections be prevented in Cocker Spaniels?

While you cannot prevent every infection due to the breed’s anatomy, you can dramatically reduce the frequency by: keeping the ears dry after water exposure, cleaning them on a routine schedule, managing allergies with your vet, and avoiding grooming mistakes like plucking hair too aggressively or using cotton swabs. Early detection of subtle symptoms also prevents minor issues from escalating into major infections.

Are Cocker Spaniels more prone to ear infections than other breeds?

Yes. Cocker Spaniels are consistently ranked as one of the top three breeds most prone to ear infections, along with Labrador Retrievers and Basset Hounds. The combination of long, heavy ear flaps and narrow, L-shaped ear canals makes them uniquely vulnerable. They also have a higher incidence of skin allergies, which is the most common underlying cause of recurrent otitis in the breed.

How do I know if my Cocker has a yeast or bacterial ear infection?

Yeast infections tend to produce a dark, waxy discharge that smells sweet or like corn chips, and the ear is usually itchy but not extremely painful. Bacterial infections tend to have a thicker, yellow or green pus, a sour or foul odor, and are often painful. Mixed infections are common in Cockers. Only your vet can confirm the type with an ear swab and microscope examination, which is important for choosing the right medication.
