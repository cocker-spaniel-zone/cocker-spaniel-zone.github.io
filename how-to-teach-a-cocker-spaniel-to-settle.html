How to Teach a Cocker Spaniel to Settle: Building an Off Switch in a Busy Spaniel That Won't Stop

To teach a Cocker Spaniel to settle, put a comfortable mat beside your chair in a quiet room. Reward your dog for going to it, then watch for a softer posture, such as a chin on the floor or a hip rolled to one side. Keep the first attempts short. Once your spaniel can relax while you sit, practise a small movement before you try walking away or settling through dinner.

How do you teach a Cocker Spaniel to settle on a mat?

Start with a mat close enough that you can deliver a reward quietly from your seat. Choose a place where the spaniel can stretch out and where people can walk past without stepping over the dog. Put the ball away, leave the television off for the first attempt and set your treats within reach before the mat goes down.

  1. Make the mat worth visiting. Let your dog look at or investigate it. Place a reward on the mat for the first approach, as in Battersea’s mat introduction exercise, then wait to see whether your dog chooses to go back. If the mat slides as your dog steps on it, pick a surface that stays put. An ordinary blanket or bed is enough if it gives your dog somewhere comfortable to lie.
  2. Reward the down quietly. If your dog already knows “down”, you can ask for it once the dog is on the mat. If they offer it first, take the opportunity. Bring the reward down to the mat so they can eat without standing or stretching towards your hand. Let your hands rest again afterwards.
  3. Notice the moment they soften. Look for a shift onto one hip, a stretched-out leg or the chin coming down. Those are signs that the relaxation exercise in Today’s Veterinary Practice suggests rewarding. Give your spaniel a chance to offer one while you sit quietly. A dog who lies down and spends the whole attempt tracking your hand still has a useful starting behaviour to build on.
  4. End while it is going well. Pick a quiet phrase such as “all done”, then give your dog room to leave. During the next attempt, change the exercise if your dog leaves before you are ready to finish. You can shorten the wait, sit nearer or take away a distraction.

Food is a tool for showing your spaniel which choice you want repeated. Keep your delivery low-key. If reaching into your pocket makes your dog spring up, set the food where you can get to it with less movement. Try offering a piece on the mat the next time your dog looks away or rests their head and then go back to what you were doing. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends reward-based methods for teaching dog behaviour.

How do you tell whether your spaniel is starting to relax?

Watch what your dog does after the reward has been swallowed and your hands are still. A dog can remain in a down while watching for the next treat. Look for a change in posture or attention, such as the chin dropping or the gaze moving away from you. The VCA coaching-calm exercise recommends rewarding those changes and placing food between the front feet.

Use the same room and the same starting position while you compare attempts.

What if your Cocker Spaniel follows every time you stand?

Practise the smallest part of standing that your spaniel can watch from the mat. That might be moving your feet under the chair, leaning forward or lifting your hips briefly. Return to your seat and give the next quiet reward on the mat if your dog is still comfortable there.

Build back towards standing in a series of ordinary actions. First put a hand on the chair arm. Next shift your weight. Once standing and sitting again is easy, turn a shoulder or take a single step, then return. If your dog comes with you when you turn towards the hallway, try the turn with your feet in place next time. Give the new action a shorter wait than a settled attempt with no movement.

Practise with the person whose movements are already easiest for the dog. If another family member is more exciting, ask them to begin seated while you run the usual exercise. When it is their turn to move, go back to a smaller action. You can be in the same room while your spaniel learns to relax through ordinary activity. Training for time alone is a separate exercise.

How do you fit settle practice into a busy spaniel’s day?

Attach it to a quiet part of a routine you already have. Try setting the mat down after the lead is put away, while you read a page on the sofa or while your computer starts up. Choose an interval when your dog has had a chance to toilet and has access to water, and when you have time to watch the first attempt.

After the walk

Finish the return-home jobs first. If your spaniel comes in looking for the ball, put the ball away and allow a quiet transition before placing the mat beside your chair. The Cocker Spaniel field planner can help you set aside separate time for sniffing, activity and settling. For a dog still absorbed in every bird outside, plan that part of the outing with the guide to managing chase around wildlife and choose a quieter moment indoors for your first attempt.

At your desk or during dinner prep

At the desk, start before the meeting. Open the laptop while the dog is already comfortable on the mat. Practise typing or reaching for a mug before asking the dog to cope with the sounds and movement of a call. In the kitchen, put the mat out of the route between the worktop and cooker. Start with a quiet task such as putting a cup down. Chopping, opening the fridge and people coming through the room can wait for later attempts.

Use a space where nobody needs to reach over your dog for food or move them off the bed. If someone in the house has noticed stiffening or guarding around food or objects, read the blog’s notes on resource guarding in Cocker Spaniels before running food-based practice in a shared area. Discuss how to set up the exercise with a qualified reward-based trainer if the reactions make it difficult to give rewards calmly.

When should you wait longer or make the exercise harder?

Increase the wait when your dog is already offering a soft posture in the current setup. Change one part of the exercise at a time. If you want to practise in a different room, begin with the mat near your chair again and reward sooner than you would in the old room.

Choose the next change before you start. Write down the room, what you will do, and which behaviour earns the next reward. Stop or simplify when the dog repeatedly leaves, scans the room or stays fixed on your hands.

Keep a short record of where you practised and what happened when you stopped actively handling the treats. If you want to record time, measure the relaxed portion of the attempt. Start when you see the soft posture and stop when your dog gets up or returns to watching you closely. Compare attempts in similar conditions. A short, loose settle beside the sofa and a longer, alert down in the kitchen answer different questions about what your spaniel can do today.

As your dog starts lying down voluntarily during the day, quietly acknowledge some of those moments. If a spoken cue is useful to your household, add “settle” as the dog begins to soften in a setup they already know. Keep using the easier version of the exercise in busier places. At home, an off switch can look as simple as your dog resting nearby while you finish what you’re doing.

Common questions about teaching a Cocker Spaniel to settle

How long should the first practice attempt last?

Start with a brief attempt you can end while your spaniel is still comfortable. If your dog can only sniff the mat and step onto it, reward that and take a break. Begin extending the wait when the dog can lie down and soften in the current setup.

Can I teach settle without a crate?

Yes. Put a blanket, bed or mat near the place where you sit. Give the dog room to get on and off. Practise while the room is quiet and make the mat an easy place to choose.

Should I keep saying settle if my dog gets up?

Use the next attempt to change something you can control. Move the mat closer, shorten the wait or make your movement smaller. Add the cue when you can predict that your dog is about to relax, so they hear the word alongside a behaviour they already offer.

What if treats make my spaniel more alert?

Keep your hands still between rewards, use a quiet delivery and put the food down at mat level. Look for a glance away or a softer posture before the next reward. If your movement towards the treat is the part that gets the dog up, practise with the treats already within easy reach.

Should I try the settle during a visit first?

Build the behaviour with familiar people in a quiet room. Then add an easier version of what happens during visits, such as someone moving in the same room while you sit close to the mat. Give your spaniel space from the doorway while you practise.
