What to Tell Your Groomer: How to Ask for the Cocker Spaniel Cut You Want (and Why Some Groomers Refuse to Shave)

To ask for the Cocker Spaniel cut you want, describe the back, ears, legs and skirt separately. Then agree on what the groomer should do if the coat can't safely be left that long. “Short and tidy” is a starting point. “A shorter body with the ear fringe left long, and a call before going shorter anywhere else” is an instruction you can both check.

What should I say if my groomer says no?

Pick what the groomer tells you. The rehearsal gives you a reply and a decision to make before you hand over the lead.

Rehearse the drop-off conversation
Could you show me the shortest finish you offer for this coat? I'd like the body easier to comb and the ear fringe left long if possible.

Before you leave. Agree on the body length and which areas keep their feathering.

A shorter finish can still have a distinct outline. In this salon demonstration, groomer Hannah Bowles shows a clipped pet trim, giving you a reference for discussing how the body and longer furnishings should look together.

How do I describe a Cocker Spaniel cut?

Describe the finished silhouette from the side, then point to the areas you want left fuller. In the American Spaniel Club's breed standard, the American Cocker has short, fine hair on the head, a medium-length body coat with undercoating and feathering on the ears, chest, belly and legs. You can use those visible differences as reference points for a pet trim.

Why do some groomers refuse to shave a Cocker Spaniel?

Ask whether the groomer is declining the requested finish, suggesting shorter work as part of mat removal, or stopping because your dog cannot safely tolerate the session. Those answers lead to different decisions.

Policies differ by provider. For example, Von Paws' grooming policy declines elective shaving of double-coated dogs and makes an exception for severe matting. Its handling policies also allow it to stop when the session becomes unsafe. Ask how your salon applies its own policy to your dog's coat and what it means by a shave. You may be asking for a short, even trim while the groomer is picturing a much closer clip.

If coat texture is the issue, show the groomer how your dog's back currently lies and ask what finish they can achieve with the approach they offer. If the coat became fluffy after a previous clip, describe where you noticed it and how you would like those areas to look.

When might the shortest cut be the practical option?

Tight mats may need to be clipped out. Ask the groomer to show you the affected areas and whether they can keep length elsewhere. VCA Animal Hospitals' coat-care guidance recommends clippers for removing mats and advises seeing a veterinarian if a mat has caused skin irritation.

Agree on a call-back point. “If you find tight mats behind the ears, please show me what can be saved. Call before a full clip-down or before shortening the rest of the coat to match.”

Tell the groomer where the comb tends to catch, including under equipment and where longer feathering folds against the body. If you notice changes where the harness sits, point to that patch at drop-off. If a shorter clip is needed, ask how the shorter patch will look beside the rest of the coat and whether blending it would mean removing length you wanted to keep.

What should we agree on before the appointment starts?

Leave with a shared description of the cut, a backup plan and a way to reach you. Use the printable cut brief to record those choices once you have talked through the parts that matter.

  • Show the length. Point at the hair on your dog or at the length in your photo. Ask the groomer to indicate their proposed length on the back, the skirt and the legs.
  • Mark what you want to keep. If the ear fringe is your favourite feature, make that explicit. Say which areas are flexible so the groomer can offer a compromise.
  • Name what happened last time. “She pulled her front feet away” gives the groomer a specific part of the session to plan for. Mention any successful breaks or handling changes you know about.
  • Set the approval limit. Agree what the groomer can adjust without a call, and what they should leave for you to decide. Confirm what happens if you miss the call.
  • Plan for pickup. Ask where the groomer found tangles and what they changed. Write down the lengths or the visible landmarks you want repeated next time.

Pick a finish that fits your routine after the appointment. If you are regularly dealing with mud in the leg feathering, tell the groomer which parts are hardest to clean and ask which can be shortened while keeping your preferred outline.

Questions to ask before a Cocker Spaniel groom

What do I say instead of “puppy cut”?

Describe what you want the dog to look like. For example, ask for a short body, tidied feet and rounded legs with the ear fringe left long. Before you go, ask the groomer to show you where they would finish the skirt.

Can I ask for a short body and long ears?

Yes. Specify the ear fringe separately from the body. Ask the groomer to check behind the ears and explain what length they can keep there. If they find a mat, decide whether they should call before changing the ear outline.

Do I need to know the clipper blade number?

Start with the finished length you want to see. Point to a photo or ask the groomer to indicate that length on your dog. If you like the result, ask what they recorded for the body so you can refer to it next time.

Should I ask for a show cut if I want the classic look?

Show the features you like, such as tidy back hair and visible leg feathering. Ask for a pet trim that keeps those features at a length you can maintain. Tell the groomer if you actually plan to show your dog so they can discuss the appropriate preparation.

What if I cannot be reached during the groom?

Agree on the fallback at drop-off. You might authorise a small adjustment to the leg fringe and ask the groomer to leave any major changes until you can speak. Check the salon’s consent and matting policies before signing.
